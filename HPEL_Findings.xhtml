Findings

Key linguistic patterns of reddit users include the widespread use of abbreviations and acronyms, the use of emojis, and the use of images and GIFs. Specialized vocabulary is also widely used, although the terminology used most often generally depends on the subreddit users are communicating within.

Abbreviations and Acronyms

Similarly to specialized language, abbreviations and acronyms are used across the website, and create substantial barriers for those who do not have previous knowledge. Some commonly used acronyms include AMA, which stands for “ask me anything,” TIL, meaning “today I learned, " and “TL;DR,” which stands for “too long; didn’t read.” AMA is often also used to refer to an event in which a user invites questions, rather than the phrase itself, which further complicates communication.

Use of Emojis

In contrast, emojis can help clarify tone, as well as the message that a user is attempting to convey. Because Reddit is an online space, users are left without the ability to interpret body language or vocal inflections, instead relying on alternative means of conveying tone, including both emojis and images. Gretchen McCuloch emphasizes this experience in her book Because Internet, stating that “Punctuation is good at representing tone of voice, but we’re still missing something, something embodied. This was the void that emoticons and emoji stepped into.” (chapter 5).

Use of Images and GIFs

In many communities, images and gifs are used as a form of communication in addition to written language. They often also carry cultural references, such as to popular movies or shows, that allow users to communicate in non-traditional ways. In some cases, written language is attached to the image or gif itself, most often when the words spoken are relevant to the current conversation or essential to the interpretation of what the commenter is attempting to say.
In other cases, the images speak for themselves.
In some subreddits, much of the primary communication is done through images, and some subreddits, such as r/ThereIsnoCat, where users post photos of cats attempting, in some cases poorly, to hide. Others within the community respond by trying to find the cat, or, on the “weekly There Is No Animal Tuesday,” to find other animals. The subreddit is built upon the posting of and conversation regarding images, and relies upon the use of both visual and textual communication for success.
By contrast, in some cases the use of images and gifs may be limited, or accompanied with irrelevant writing so as to meet a required word count set by the moderators of the individual community. r/howislivingthere, for example, is focused on providing community members with genuine information regarding the day-to-day experiences of living in various places across the globe, and their rules state that “if all you have to say about living in a place is a one-word answer you should keep it to yourself” (Posting Guidelines). Additionally, the subreddit moderators state that “photos are welcome and encouraged if they show real daily life,” although users are required to “add context in the caption,” leading to the inclusion of a word count requirement within the rules. However, not all images used within conversation are meant to directly answer a question posed regarding daily life in a specific location, and users who wish to use images or gifs for other purposes are required to include other commentary to post their comment.
This commentary, along with everything else on the subreddit, is also required to be written in English, which is particularly interesting due to the intentionally global nature of the community, as well as the moderators’ aim to hear only from “people who currently live in the place they are posting about” or “people who have lived there long enough to speak from experience” (Posting Guidelines). As such, experts on a specific region are likely to be excluded from a conversation in which their insights could potentially be very helpful.
